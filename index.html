Case study

The book that can't be cooked.

Daftari — savings groups where the chama is a wallet and the chain is the ledger.

The problem

Rotating savings groups — chamas — run on trust and a book. Members pay in on a schedule; each round, one member takes the pot. It works because everyone knows each other, and because one person keeps the record. That record is usually paper, a spreadsheet, or a WhatsApp thread. It is also the thing people argue about.

The design

A chama in Daftari is a real Kaspa wallet, not a database row. Members are wallets that pay into it. Every contribution and withdrawal is a real transaction: built server-side, signed in the browser with the Kastle wallet, and broadcast to testnet-10.

Membership is self-enforcing. A wallet can join only after it has actually transacted with the chama's wallet. Social state — names, memberships, sign-in challenges — lives in a hosted Turso database. The money never does.

If the record can be edited, it isn't a record.

What it took

The hard part wasn't the transaction. It was making an on-chain group feel like a group: joining, being recognized, seeing who paid and who didn't, without turning the page into a block explorer. Mainnet readiness is less about code and more about edges — wallet onboarding for people who have never held a key, dispute flows that don't require me, and the moment a chama grows past the people who already trust each other.

That's the honest test. Not whether the transaction confirms — whether the group still works when it isn't a group of friends.
