Who I am

Born in Rehovot. Happier in the desert.

Not a CV. The short version of how I got here, and what I care about.

I was born and raised in Rehovot. After high school I did a mechina in Akko — a year to work out what I actually wanted — then the Paratroopers, where I became a combat commander. Reichman University came next, computer science, and then Cognyte, building backend systems that other people's work depended on.

The timeline isn't the interesting part. What I'd tell you first is that I'm curious about almost everything, and curiosity tends to take me off the map. A desert over a beach, a trail that isn't on the map, a place with no sign at the entrance. I hike a lot, and the further from the parking lot the better.

Off the trail it's bachata and yoga, and whatever I'm reading about AI and blockchain that month. I build my own things — five apps, self-custody tools on Kaspa — because making something is more interesting than waiting for someone else to.

Underneath all of it is one idea: teach a person to fish. Don't hand someone a product they need permission to use — give them the thing itself. That's why the money in my apps moves between people instead of through a platform balance, why you sign in with a key you hold, and why this is a hand-written page instead of a profile on someone else's feed.

And I don't wait for perfect. Progress beats perfection — shipping something real and then making it better is how everything here actually got built.

Right now I'm reading about AI and blockchain, and looking for the next trail nobody's on.

My own corner of the internet. No feed, no algorithm, no one else's rules.

Case study

The book that can't be cooked.

Daftari — savings groups where the chama is a wallet and the chain is the ledger.

The problem

Rotating savings groups — chamas — run on trust and a book. Members pay in on a schedule; each round, one member takes the pot. It works because everyone knows each other, and because one person keeps the record. That record is usually paper, a spreadsheet, or a WhatsApp thread. It is also the thing people argue about.

The design

A chama in Daftari is a real Kaspa wallet, not a database row. Members are wallets that pay into it. Every contribution and withdrawal is a real transaction: built server-side, signed in the browser with the Kastle wallet, and broadcast to testnet-10.

Membership is self-enforcing. A wallet can join only after it has actually transacted with the chama's wallet. Social state — names, memberships, sign-in challenges — lives in a hosted Turso database. The money never does.

If the record can be edited, it isn't a record.

What it took

The hard part wasn't the transaction. It was making an on-chain group feel like a group: joining, being recognized, seeing who paid and who didn't, without turning the page into a block explorer. Mainnet readiness is less about code and more about edges — wallet onboarding for people who have never held a key, dispute flows that don't require me, and the moment a chama grows past the people who already trust each other.

That's the honest test. Not whether the transaction confirms — whether the group still works when it isn't a group of friends.
