Startup notes

The book that can't be cooked.

A startup around savings groups in East Africa. I built the MVP, did the research, and stopped — the problem was real, my solution wasn't native enough.

RoleResearch · Product · Full-stack StageMVP, stopped StackTypeScript · Express · Turso · Kaspa

Where it started

At a Nura Global hackathon, a conversation about payments in Africa turned to the chama: a rotating savings group where members pay in on a schedule and one takes the pot each round. I read about it, spoke to friends who know the market, and decided it was worth researching.

The problem

A chama runs on trust and a book. That book is usually paper, a spreadsheet, or a WhatsApp thread — and it's the thing people argue about.

The design

A chama in Daftari is a real Kaspa wallet, not a database row. Members pay in on-chain, signed in the browser with Kastle. A wallet can join only after it has transacted with the group. Names and memberships live in a database; the money never does.

If the record can be edited, it isn't a record.

What I couldn't solve

  • Privacy. A record no one can edit is also a record everyone can read. A chama's book belongs to its members, not the world.
  • The edges. Onboarding people who have never held a key, disputes that don't require me, and groups that outgrow the people who already trust each other.

Why I stopped

Adoption. A chama is a group of people who already trust each other, and most would rather keep the system they know than move their money to an app built by an outsider. I wasn't part of that trust, and trust doesn't move because the code is better.

What I took from it

The lesson wasn't technical. A record can be made trustworthy; a relationship can't be coded. Next time I'd start with the group and its trust, not with the chain.
